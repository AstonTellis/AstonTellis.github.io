What Snowden Actually Taught Us — And Why Most People Got the Wrong Lesson

Aston Tellis
December 2024
8 min read

In 2013, Edward Snowden handed journalists a hard drive that proved something most people suspected but couldn't confirm: governments were building surveillance infrastructure that covered the entire internet. Eleven years later, most people's reaction to this is a shrug. That shrug is itself the most important data point in the story.

The popular narrative is that Snowden was heroic but his revelations changed nothing. That narrative is wrong — it changed everything, just not in ways that show up in opinion polls. HTTPS became the default on almost all websites. End-to-end encryption became mainstream. Signal exists and has hundreds of millions of users. The EU passed GDPR. These weren't coincidences.

What Snowden actually revealed wasn't just that governments spy — everyone knew that. He revealed the architecture. The specific programs (PRISM, XKeyscore), the specific companies involved, the specific legal frameworks that made it all "legal." That specificity mattered, because vague knowledge is easy to dismiss and specific knowledge demands a response.

"Arguing that you don't care about the right to privacy because you have nothing to hide is no different from saying you don't care about free speech because you have nothing to say."
— Edward Snowden

As someone studying CS and Business, I think about Snowden's revelations differently than most people. I see them as a product failure. Governments built surveillance infrastructure with no accountability, no user consent, and no oversight. If any commercial product worked this way — collecting data without consent, sharing it without disclosure — it would rightly be called a scandal. The lesson for anyone building technology: design for the user's interests first, not for your own ability to collect.

Why I'm Selective About Social Media — And How I Stay Connected Without It

5 min

I do have a LinkedIn profile and use it selectively, but I'm intentional about how much I share on platforms whose business model depends on data collection. Understanding the business model helps you use these tools more thoughtfully — as a feature, not a flaw.

Facebook's revenue is approximately $50 per user per year in the US. That money doesn't come from you paying for the service — it comes from advertisers paying for access to your attention, your data, and your behavioural profile. The more you engage, the more data they have, the more they can charge. That's not a conspiracy — it's in their annual report.

The LinkedIn Problem

LinkedIn is trickier because professional networking is a genuinely useful concept. But LinkedIn is owned by Microsoft, runs an advertising business, and has had several significant data breaches. More subtly: it normalises the idea that your professional identity should live on a platform you don't control.

"If you're not paying for the product, you are the product." — The oldest rule on the internet.

Finding the Balance

I maintain a LinkedIn profile for professional networking and use email for direct communication. For personal browsing I prefer tools with stronger privacy defaults — not because social media is bad, but because choice and awareness matter. Being intentional about your digital presence is a skill, not a stance.

How Open Banking is Quietly Revolutionising Financial Privacy

6 min

Open Banking sounds like it should be a privacy nightmare — sharing your transaction data with third-party apps. In practice, it might be the first time financial data regulation actually gave consumers meaningful control. Here's why the EU got this one right.

Under PSD2 (the EU Payment Services Directive), banks must share your transaction data with third-party apps — but only if you explicitly give consent, only for the specific data requested, and you can revoke that consent at any time. This is privacy-by-design built into regulation.

Why This Matters for FinTech

The traditional financial data model was opaque: banks knew everything about you, you knew almost nothing about how they used it, and you had no meaningful way to switch. Open Banking breaks that monopoly. It's not perfect, but it's the right direction.

The Counterargument

Critics argue that "consent fatigue" means users click through permissions without reading them, making consent meaningless. This is a real problem. The solution isn't less sharing — it's better consent design. Shorter, simpler, more specific permission requests with real explanations of consequences.

This is exactly the kind of problem I want to work on: where technology, regulation, and user experience intersect.

AI and Privacy: The Conversation We're Not Having

7 min

The public conversation about AI safety focuses on existential risk — could it become sentient, could it destroy humanity. The boring but more immediate risk barely gets discussed: AI is the most powerful surveillance tool ever built, and we're deploying it at scale without serious governance.

Consider what modern LLMs actually require: enormous training datasets that often include scraped personal information, deployment through cloud platforms that log your queries, fine-tuning on user feedback that further expands data collection. None of this is secret — it's in the terms of service that nobody reads.

The Scale Problem

Legacy surveillance required dedicated resources — people to watch people. AI-powered surveillance is near-zero marginal cost. Facial recognition at scale, behaviour prediction from aggregated data, real-time content monitoring — these were science fiction twenty years ago. They're off-the-shelf products now.

The question isn't whether AI will be used for surveillance. It will be, and it is. The question is what governance structures exist to limit that use — and whether we build them before or after the damage is done.

What Good Looks Like

The EU AI Act is imperfect but directionally correct. It bans real-time biometric surveillance in public spaces, requires transparency about AI decision-making in high-risk contexts, and mandates human oversight. These aren't technical solutions — they're governance solutions. That's what the problem requires.

The Honest Case for Studying CS and Business Together

4 min

Most people think combined degrees are a hedge — you can't decide so you do both. I think they're a strategy. The most interesting problems in technology right now are not technical problems. They're organisational, ethical, and strategic ones.

A pure CS graduate understands how to build the system but often struggles to explain why it should be built, or whether it's the right system. A pure business graduate can articulate the why perfectly but often can't engage meaningfully with engineers about the how. Both are useful. Neither is as useful as someone who can do both, even if imperfectly.

Where This Shows Up in Practice

Product managers need to write user stories that engineers can act on. Business analysts need to query databases directly rather than waiting for reports. FinTech analysts need to understand APIs and payment architectures to spot opportunities. None of this requires being an expert engineer — it requires being technically literate enough to operate in a technical environment.

That's what I'm building: not expertise in two fields, but fluency in the space between them.
